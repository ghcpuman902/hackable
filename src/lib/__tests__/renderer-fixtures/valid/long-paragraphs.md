**You look like a crypto worker, and scammers who copy real wallet projects are likely to aim at someone with your public profile and your habit of connecting a wallet to new sites.**[^1]

Priya Venkataraman lists a wallet company on her profile, posts about new token launches most weeks, and has an email address that appears in an older breach. Together those details give a scammer an easy story to build a convincing message around. A message that names her employer and a project she follows would look far more believable than a generic one. She would be likely to open it quickly, because it appears to come from someone in her own field of work.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Wallet drainer sites copy real projects so that one signature empties the wallet behind it, and the fake pages are often live for only a few hours before they are replaced.** A page that asks for a signature the moment you connect is the usual warning sign, because a genuine project rarely needs one before you have chosen to do anything.[^2] Scammers send the link in a direct message that appears to come from a colleague or a community moderator, and they time it to a real launch so that the rush feels normal and checking feels unnecessary.[^3]

> [!TIP]
> **Use a hardware wallet and read every signature request.** If a site rushes you, close it.
